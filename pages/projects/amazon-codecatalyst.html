Context

Amazon CodeCatalyst is a developer platform where issues, planning, and pull requests translate directly into production code. This work focused on integrating Amazon Q into these workflows in a way that supports automation without eroding trust, ownership, or review discipline.

The core challenge was not introducing AI capability, but determining how Amazon Q should participate in developer workflows: when it should act independently, how its progress should be communicated, and where human review must remain explicit.

The design goal: make Amazon Q feel like a reliable teammate—working in the background, visible when it matters, and always accountable to human review.

My responsibility

  • Design AI-assisted workflows for issue creation, task execution, and pull request review
  • Define interaction patterns that balance visibility, trust, and cognitive load
  • Design failure states and recovery paths without breaking developer flow
  • Ensure AI actions remain reviewable, reversible, and clearly attributable
  • Partner closely with product, engineering, and applied science teams

Initiative 1: Assigning issues and epics to Amazon Q

Issue creation is a natural entry point for AI assistance: developers already express intent, but translating that intent into structured, executable work takes time. The opportunity was to allow developers to assign issues and epics directly to Amazon Q to generate plans and code—without overwhelming users with implementation details.

A key design tension emerged early: the more internal AI data we exposed, the less likely developers were to validate the output. Too little visibility felt opaque; too much detail reduced trust and slowed adoption. The design challenge was to surface just enough information to support confidence and review.

Activity feed as a trust surface

Once Amazon Q is assigned work, developers need a clear way to understand what the AI is doing without monitoring every step. The activity feed was designed as a dedicated trust surface—summarizing intent, progress, and decision points rather than exposing raw execution data.

This approach acknowledges an important behavioral insight: developers are more likely to review and validate AI output when the system communicates progress in structured, human-readable terms.

Designing for failure without breaking momentum

AI-assisted workflows must assume failure as a first-class state. When Amazon Q cannot proceed—due to missing context, permissions, or conflicts—the experience should be explicit, calm, and forward-looking.

Instead of treating failures as errors to hide, the UI focuses on preserving momentum: clearly explaining what happened, what was affected, and what the developer can do next.

Initiative 2: Amazon Q in the pull request workflow

Pull requests are already information-dense environments. Introducing AI support here required careful restraint: assistance needed to reduce review effort without adding noise or obscuring accountability.

Amazon Q was designed to support repetitive, cognitively heavy tasks—such as summarizing comment threads, drafting PR descriptions, and proposing updates based on reviewer feedback— while keeping the source of truth firmly anchored in code and discussion.

Initiative 3: Subscription controls, limits, and transparency

AI capabilities in enterprise tools come with usage limits, permissions, and identity context. These constraints are part of the experience and must be understandable—not hidden.

The goal was to design controls that feel predictable and empowering rather than restrictive, allowing teams to adopt AI responsibly while maintaining clarity around access and cost.

Results and impact

Integrating Amazon Q into CodeCatalyst shifted AI from an abstract capability to a visible, reviewable participant in developer workflows. Developers could see what the system was doing, why it was doing it, and when human input was required.

The activity feed established a clear trust surface, failure states preserved momentum, and pull request summaries reduced orientation cost without weakening accountability. Together, these patterns created a scalable model for embedding generative AI into developer tools—where automation accelerates work but responsibility remains human.
